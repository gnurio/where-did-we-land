Where did we land?

Point it at a meeting transcript and it writes one self-contained HTML page: a topic timeline showing what got picked back up, a ledger of where every thread landed with the verbatim line that proves it, the loops left open, the commitments, and who held the floor.

Heat (1995), the diner scene

Two men who intend to kill each other split the airtime 55/45, thirteen turns each, and settle four threads out of eight. Three subjects get raised, dropped and picked back up, and the two they circle hardest are the two neither man closes. The best-balanced and least conclusive conversation you will ever watch — and a fair picture of what most meetings look like once you count them.

Styled in a noir palette drawn from the film's posters, and with a seventy-four-second video at the foot of the page: the scene on the left, this ledger assembling on the right as they talk.

What is on the page, and what is not

Counts are measured; states are inferred. Turns, timings and word counts come straight from the transcript. Every state is a judgment, so each one carries the line it was read from — check the quote before you act on it.

The file stores word counts, not words. A turn is [speaker, seconds, wordCount], so the charts are exact while the transcript never leaves your machine. The only verbatim text is the dozen or so lines chosen as receipts. That is what makes a ledger safe to send to someone who was not in the room.
